# Visual direction working notes

## Seven grounded systems, ordered by audience resonance

1. A cram-school workbook marked in red pencil: the user recognizes the study task immediately; wrong answers and explanations already belong in its grammar.
2. A study-guideline proof sheet: official section numbers, editorial hierarchy, page references, and corrections map cleanly to source-linked topic practice.
3. A question filing drawer: retrieval and wrong-question collection are familiar as ordered labels, with risk of becoming a card-heavy UI.
4. A library accession register: source provenance and topic classification fit, but can feel archival instead of active.
5. A rail timetable and transfer map: the monthly study route is legible, but the transit metaphor adds decoding cost.
6. A study-guide concept map: it shows how syllabus ideas connect, but could make learners decode a visual metaphor before reading the question.
7. Exam-room instruction signage: a fixed question number, clear response area, timer, and mark-for-review index make timed practice feel orderly; the risk is making daily learning feel too institutional.

The direction seed assigned candidate 7, exam-room instruction signage. Candidate 1 is the grounded pick because its audience identification and product clarity are stronger. The assigned direction is kept viable by using the signage rules for real wayfinding, question numbering, timer priority, and mark-for-review states rather than for decoration.

## Challenger fusions and verdicts

- Depot destination blind: declined. Its fixed index could help question navigation; the fabric roll and clipped moving labels do not help a reader study. Keep a stable, clearly located question index.
- Flight instrument six-pack: declined. Its cross-check hierarchy is useful for the dashboard; six gauges distract from the learner's actual questions. Keep immediate visibility for the key study state.
- Industrial streetwear grammar: declined. Its active/disabled states can be legible; quote-wrapped labels and warning stripes misstate a calm study task. Keep high-contrast state changes.
- Deep-sea dive profile: declined. Its vertical path could represent study progression; the ocean framing asks users to learn a second metaphor. Keep topic order and each question's location in the guide.
- Doujin event catalog: competitive. A compact numbered index can make the question pool tangible; use semantic chapter groups and readable rows instead of thumbnails without content.
- Designers Republic sleeve: declined. Its density discipline could support precise source codes; noise and faux-official marks would undermine trust. Keep exact source and question identifiers.
